Sources describe **sensitivity to initial conditions** as a defining characteristic of **Chaos Theory**, used to explain why dynamic systems often develop in unpredictable ways. Within the broader context of system characteristics, this sensitivity is the primary reason that long-term outcomes cannot be precisely determined.

### Context within Chaos Theory

The sources list sensitivity to initial conditions as the first of four fundamental **characteristics of Chaos**:

- **Sensitivity to initial conditions:** Small changes at the start lead to massive, unpredictable differences as the system progresses.
- **Mixing:** Points that start near each other can end up in completely different positions.
- **Non-linearity:** A system where the output of one stage becomes the input for the next.
- **Feedback:** This serves as the major driver of chaotic behavior.

### Predictability vs. Determinism

In a **linear system**, the process is deterministic; if you know the initial conditions, you can accurately predict the outcome. However, the sources emphasize that real-world systems rarely operate in such a steady state. Because systems are generally **non-linear**, they do not maintain the same shape they had when they commenced.

### Practical Implications

This sensitivity ensures that while a system's general "genotype" (general form) might be predictable, its "phenotype" (specific shape) is not. This explains the **uniqueness** of natural and artificial entities:

- **Nature:** No two trees, snowflakes, or people are exactly alike because their development is sensitive to starting parameters and environmental feedback.
- **Technology:** Even two cars of the same make and model will develop different patterns of wear and failure over time.

Ultimately, understanding sensitivity to initial conditions helps systems thinkers move away from **linear thinking**—which seeks a single cause for a single effect—and instead accept that systems are dynamic, evolving unpredictably through time.
