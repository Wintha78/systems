Sources describe **mixing** as one of the four fundamental characteristics of **Chaos Theory**, providing a mechanism for why dynamic systems develop in unpredictable ways. Within the larger context of system characteristics, mixing explains how order can exist within what appears to be disorder.

### Definition and Mechanism

Mixing refers to a process where **adjacent points** (points that start very close to one another) may end up in **completely different positions** as the system progresses through time. This ensures that even if a system has a predictable general form, its specific internal arrangements evolve unpredictably.

### Context within Chaos Theory

The sources place mixing alongside three other core characteristics that define chaotic behavior in systems:

- **Sensitivity to initial conditions:** Small starting differences lead to massive eventual changes.
- **Mixing:** Nearby points diverge to distant locations.
- **Non-linearity:** The output of one stage becomes the input for the next, creating complex cycles.
- **Feedback:** The primary driver of chaotic behavior.

### Significance in Systems Thinking

In the broader context of **Thinking in Systems**, mixing illustrates why systems are **dynamic** rather than static.

- **Order vs. Disorder:** While each component of a system (like individual trees in a forest) may end up in a unique "disordered" position due to mixing, the system as a whole remains within predictable bounds.
- **Unpredictability:** Mixing is a reason why we cannot reliably indicate exactly what will happen in complex emotional, social, or economic processes.
- **Evolution of Form:** Because of mixing and non-linearity, systems do not move through time with the exact same shape they had when they commenced.

Ultimately, understanding mixing helps a systems thinker recognize that **complexity** is not just a lack of information, but a structural feature of how dynamic systems reorganize themselves over time.
