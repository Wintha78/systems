Sources describe the **unreflective thinker** as the initial stage in the journey toward improving one’s cognitive abilities. This stage is characterized by a fundamental **lack of awareness** regarding the significant problems or pitfalls inherent in one's own thinking processes.

### The Context of the Six Stages of Improvement

According to the sources, developing the skill of critical thinking is a gradual process that requires moving through six distinct stages:

- **Stage 1: The Unreflective Thinker:** You are unaware of the pitfalls in your thinking.
- **Stage 2:** You start developing awareness of problems in your thinking.
- **Stage 3:** You begin working on thought processes, though not regularly.
- **Stage 4:** You recognize the necessity of regular practice.
- **Stage 5:** You begin to notice tangible changes in how you think.
- **Stage 6:** You develop the ability to be consistently insightful.

### Characteristics of the Unreflective Thinker

The sources highlight that **"most of us are stuck in this stage"**. Key traits of an unreflective thinker include:

- **Living on "Autopilot":** <mark style="background:#b1ffff">Many people in this stage operate without intention, merely reacting to their environment </mark>rather than directing their thoughts.
- **Unconscious Biases:** Because they do not reflect on their mental processes, these thinkers are often controlled by **paradigms**—a blend of others' opinions and habits—without even being aware of it.
- **Reliance on System 1 Thinking:** Unreflective thinking aligns with "System 1," which is fast, intuitive, and automatic but highly susceptible to errors and cognitive biases.
- **Taking Thinking for Granted:** As long as a person takes their thinking for granted, they cannot unlock their true potential or identify the "unforced errors" that lead to poor decisions.

### Moving Beyond Stage 1

The transition from an unreflective thinker to a more advanced stage requires a **"conscious commitment to learning"** and hard work. To progress, an individual must first accept that there are specific flaws in how they currently think and then put in a deliberate effort to improve.

The sources suggest that this growth is often triggered by "disorienting dilemmas" or crisis situations that force a person to re-examine their existing mental models and adopt a more flexible, reflective mindset. Techniques such as **self-examination**, maintaining an **intellectual journal**, and practicing **intentional thinking** are cited as essential tools for moving past this initial, unreflective stage.
